Best Pool Accessories Worth Actually Owning

What genuinely earns a place in the pool shed, what's marketed hard but rarely worth it, and five accessories worth considering.

What actually earns a place in the pool shed

Pool accessory aisles are full of products, but the genuinely useful ones cluster into three jobs: accurately testing the water, physically cleaning what circulation and the main filter miss, and monitoring conditions so problems get caught early rather than after they're visible. Everything in this guide does one of those three jobs — nothing here is a novelty or a "nice to have" bundled in to pad out a kit.

Testing tools

Nothing else on this list matters if you're dosing chemicals based on inaccurate readings. This is the category worth spending the most on relative to its price, since the cost difference between a basic and a good test kit is small compared to how much it affects every other maintenance decision.

Test strips
Fast and cheap, fine for a quick mid-week check, but noticeably less precise than a reagent kit — particularly for chlorine and pH, the two numbers that matter most day to day. Good as a supplement, not as your only testing method.
Liquid reagent test kits
The type most pool professionals use behind the counter — drop-based color-matching tests that give meaningfully more accurate readings than strips, especially for distinguishing free chlorine from combined chlorine (chloramines), which strips often can't separate reliably. Worth the extra cost for anyone managing their own pool chemistry seriously. See our chemistry cheat sheet for what to actually do with the readings.
Digital/electronic testers
Convenient and precise for the specific parameters they measure, but typically cover fewer parameters than a full liquid kit and cost more — a reasonable middle ground rather than a full replacement for a comprehensive reagent kit.

Cleaning tools

Even with a robotic cleaner or a well-run automatic system, a few hand tools genuinely earn their place for jobs automation doesn't fully cover.

Telescopic pole
The single most versatile accessory here — a good pole accepts a skimmer net, brush, or vacuum head, covering surface debris, spot-brushing, and manual vacuuming with one handle. Worth buying a genuinely sturdy one; a flimsy pole that flexes under load is a common source of frustration.
Skimmer net
Still the fastest way to grab leaves and floating debris off the surface before they sink and decompose, releasing tannins and organic material that adds to chlorine demand. A quick daily skim is one of the highest-value-for-effort maintenance habits.
Wall and floor brush
Brushing walls and steps weekly disrupts algae before it can establish and get a visible foothold, and reaches corners and the waterline that a robotic cleaner or the main filtration cycle alone often misses.

Monitoring tools

Floating or digital thermometer
Simple but genuinely useful for anyone running a heater — confirms it's actually holding target temperature, and catches a malfunctioning heater or heat pump early rather than only noticing once the water feels off. See our pool heaters guide if you're deciding whether to add one.
Filter pressure gauge
Usually built into the filter system already, but worth confirming it's easy to read and functioning — this is the main early-warning sign that a filter needs cleaning, covered in our pool filters guide.

Chemical handling and storage accessories

Chemical storage bins or cabinets
Keeping pool chemicals in a dedicated, ventilated storage space away from direct sun and out of reach of children and pets isn't just a convenience — several common pool chemicals degrade faster with heat and humidity exposure, and different chemical types stored too close together carry a genuine reaction risk if a container fails.
Chemical-safe gloves and eyewear
Worth keeping on hand specifically for handling concentrated shock, acid, or stabilizer products — cheap, low-effort protection against the splash and dust exposure that granular and liquid pool chemicals both carry.
A dedicated mixing bucket
Used only for pre-dissolving granular chemicals like cal-hypo shock before adding to the pool — see our shocking guide for why this step matters. Keep it separate from any bucket used for other cleaning tasks.

Accessories for automated cleaning setups

If you already run a robotic pool cleaner, a few smaller accessories extend and protect that investment rather than duplicating its job.

Caddy or storage cart

Keeps a robotic cleaner off the ground between uses, extends the power cord's working life by reducing kinks and strain, and speeds up drying — genuinely useful rather than a novelty for anyone running a robot regularly.

Spare filter cartridges/bags

Robotic cleaner filter bags or cartridges need regular emptying and periodic replacement — keeping a spare on hand avoids a gap in cleaning coverage while a clogged one is being washed and dried.

Replacement brushes and tracks

Wear parts on a robotic cleaner, particularly the drive tracks and scrubbing brushes, degrade with regular use — checking for manufacturer-specific replacement parts before a season starts avoids a mid-season cleaning gap while waiting on a part to arrive.

Accessories for saltwater pools specifically

Salt pools share most of the same accessory needs as chlorine pools, but a couple of items are worth adding specifically.

Salt tester
A dedicated salt level test — either strips, a digital tester, or included as one function of a broader reagent kit — checks the salt concentration the chlorinator cell needs to actually produce chlorine, a number a standard chlorine/pH kit doesn't cover on its own.
Cell cleaning tool
Salt chlorinator cells accumulate scale over time, particularly in higher-calcium-hardness water, and periodic cleaning (often with a mild acid solution per the manufacturer's specific instructions) keeps the cell producing chlorine at its rated capacity rather than gradually underperforming.

See our salt vs chlorine guide for how salt system maintenance compares to manual chlorine dosing more broadly.

What's marketed hard but rarely worth it

Chemical floaters as a primary sanitizing method

Fine as a slow-release supplement between manual doses, but relying on one as the sole chlorine source makes dosing imprecise and hard to adjust for changing conditions — a floater can't respond to a sudden bather-load spike or heavy rain the way a deliberate, tested dose can.

Novelty water-testing gadgets with no reagent or strip component

Several products on the market claim to test water chemistry through electronic sensors alone with no consumable reagent — treat marketing claims here skeptically and cross-check against a real reagent or strip test before trusting an unfamiliar device for dosing decisions.

Oversized all-in-one kits with items you'll never use

Bundled kits often pad out the box with items like patch kits or low-grade test strips that a specific pool may never need. A kit is a reasonable starting point for a brand-new pool owner, but check what's actually inside against what you genuinely need before assuming more items means better value.

Seasonal and off-season accessories

Winterizing plugs and air pillows
For climates that close the pool over winter, expansion plugs in skimmer and return lines plus an air pillow under the winter cover prevent ice damage from freezing water expanding in the plumbing. See our closing a pool guide for the full sequence.
Cover pump
A small submersible pump that clears rain and melted snow off a winter cover — genuinely useful in climates with real winter precipitation, since standing water on a cover adds weight and strain the anchoring system isn't necessarily built for.
Solar cover reel
Makes daily use of a solar cover far more likely to actually happen — a cover that's a hassle to remove and replace tends to get left off, defeating its purpose. Covered in more detail in our pool covers guide.

Safety accessories worth taking seriously

These aren't optional convenience items — if there are children, pets, or non-swimmers around the pool regularly, this category matters more than anything else on this page.

Pool alarm

A surface-wave or sub-surface pool alarm detects unexpected entry into the water and sounds an alert — a genuinely useful additional layer of protection, though never a substitute for active supervision or a proper physical barrier.

Rated safety cover

The only cover type that should be relied on to support real weight — a standard solar or winter cover is not built for this and shouldn't be treated as if it is. Covered in detail in our pool covers guide.

Life ring or reaching pole

A dedicated rescue ring or pole kept poolside, separate from cleaning equipment, is standard practice around any residential pool and required by code in many jurisdictions — check local regulations if you're unsure what's mandatory where you are.

Budgeting for a full accessory setup

Bare essentials
A basic test kit or strips, a telescopic pole with a skimmer net, and a thermometer covers the genuine minimum for testing and surface cleaning — the lowest-cost starting point that still lets you actually manage the pool rather than guess.
Well-equipped setup
Adding a proper liquid reagent test kit, a dedicated wall/floor brush, and a chemical storage bin rounds out a setup that covers testing, cleaning, and safe chemical handling without significant redundancy.
Full setup with automated cleaning support
On top of the above, spare filter cartridges or bags and a storage caddy for a robotic cleaner round out a setup for an owner running full automation and wanting to avoid mid-season maintenance gaps.

None of this needs to be bought all at once — most owners build up this list gradually as gaps become obvious, rather than buying everything upfront. The one exception worth prioritizing early regardless of budget is accurate testing, since it affects every other maintenance decision from day one.

Building a maintenance routine around these tools

Owning the right accessories only pays off if they're actually used on a consistent schedule — a good telescopic pole sitting unused in the shed does nothing for water quality. A simple routine most owners settle into:

  1. Daily or every other day: skim

    A quick pass with a skimmer net catches surface debris before it sinks and starts decomposing — the single fastest habit for keeping water looking clear day to day.

  2. 2-3 times a week: test

    Chlorine and pH, using either strips for a quick check or a reagent kit for the more accurate reading — see our chemistry cheat sheet for exact target ranges and what to do with the result.

  3. Weekly: brush

    Walls, steps, and waterline tile, disrupting any algae before it can establish a visible foothold — a job automation often misses even with a robotic cleaner running regularly.

  4. Weekly: check filter pressure

    A quick glance at the pressure gauge catches a filter that needs cleaning before it becomes a circulation or clarity problem — see our pool filters guide for what the reading means.

  5. Monthly: full water test

    Calcium hardness and cyanuric acid, which drift more slowly and don't need the twice-weekly attention chlorine and pH do.

None of this requires expensive equipment — it's mostly about having the right basic tools within reach and actually using them on a schedule, rather than reaching for them only once a problem is already visible.

Common questions

What's the one accessory every pool owner actually needs?

A proper liquid reagent test kit, not just test strips. Every other maintenance decision — dosing, troubleshooting, knowing when to shock — depends on accurate readings, and strips alone are noticeably less precise for chlorine and pH than a reagent kit.

Do I need a telescopic pole if I already have a robotic cleaner?

Yes — a robotic cleaner handles the pool floor and walls, but a pole with a skimmer net or brush attachment is still the fastest way to grab surface leaves before they sink, brush stubborn spots between full cleaning cycles, and reach the waterline tile a robot can't scrub.

Are floating pool toys and inflatables worth including in a maintenance kit?

That's a personal/recreational purchase rather than a maintenance one, so it's outside the scope of this guide — everything here is chosen for a genuine functional role in testing, cleaning, or monitoring the pool, not general pool-day fun items.

Is an all-in-one accessory kit worth it over buying items separately?

For a new pool owner outfitting from scratch, yes — a kit is usually cheaper than buying each piece individually and covers the basics in one purchase. Owners who already have most pieces, or who want higher-quality individual items (like a proper reagent test kit instead of the strips a bundle usually includes), are often better off buying separately.
