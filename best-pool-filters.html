Best Pool Filters: Sand vs Cartridge vs DE

The three filter types explained honestly — filtration quality, maintenance effort, and running cost — plus five filters worth considering.

The three filter types, honestly compared

Every residential pool filter is one of three types, and each represents a genuinely different trade-off between how fine the filtration is, how much hands-on maintenance it needs, and what it costs to keep running. There's no universally "best" one — the right choice depends on which of those three factors matters most to you.

Sand filters
Water passes through a bed of specially graded sand, which traps debris down to around 20–40 microns. Lowest maintenance of the three — backwash periodically, replace the sand every 5–7 years — and the cheapest to run long-term since there's no recurring cartridge or DE cost. The trade-off is the least fine filtration of the three types.
Cartridge filters
Water passes through a pleated fabric cartridge that traps debris down to around 10–20 microns — finer than sand. No backwashing needed (which also saves water), just periodic rinsing and eventual cartridge replacement. Generally considered the best balance of the three for most residential owners.
DE (diatomaceous earth) filters
Water passes through grids coated in DE powder — a fine, naturally occurring mineral — trapping particles down to as small as 2–5 microns, noticeably finer than either alternative. This gives the clearest water of the three types, but DE filters need the most hands-on maintenance: backwashing plus recharging fresh DE powder after each one, and the powder itself is an ongoing cost.

Which one actually suits your situation

  • Want the lowest ongoing cost and least fuss — sand is the simplest, cheapest long-term option, and perfectly adequate water clarity for most pools.
  • Want the best all-round balance — cartridge filters suit the majority of residential owners: finer filtration than sand, less hands-on work than DE, no backwashing.
  • Want the clearest possible water and don't mind extra maintenance — DE gives genuinely superior clarity, chosen by owners (and some pool professionals) who prioritize water quality above convenience.
  • Water-conscious or in a drought-restricted area — cartridge filters don't require backwashing at all, unlike sand or DE, which both waste water each time they're cleaned.

What each one actually costs to run

Sand filter running cost
Lowest of the three — sand media itself is inexpensive and lasts 5–7 years before replacement, with no recurring consumable cost between backwashes.
Cartridge filter running cost
Moderate — cartridges themselves cost more upfront than a bag of sand, and typically need replacing every 1–2 years depending on pool use and how consistently they're rinsed in between.
DE filter running cost
Highest — DE powder is a genuine recurring cost added after every backwash, on top of the filter grids themselves eventually needing replacement.
Water cost (backwashing)
Sand and DE filters both use a meaningful amount of water each time they're backwashed — worth factoring in for owners in water-restricted regions, where cartridge's no-backwash design becomes a real practical advantage rather than just a convenience.

Sizing a filter correctly

A filter has to be matched to your pump's flow rate (measured in gallons per minute) and your pool's total volume — an undersized filter can't keep up with the water being pushed through it, reducing filtration quality and shortening the filter's working life from constant strain. As a rough baseline, a filter should be able to turn over the pool's entire water volume at least once every 8 hours. Check both your pump's actual GPM output and the filter's rated flow capacity before buying — a mismatch between the two, in either direction, undersells what you're paying for.

Micron ratings: what they actually mean in practice

Micron rating measures the smallest particle size a filter reliably traps — a lower number means finer filtration. It's tempting to assume the finest possible filtration is always best, but there are real trade-offs worth understanding rather than just chasing the lowest number available.

Why finer isn't automatically better
A finer filter (like DE) restricts flow more than a coarser one at the same pressure, meaning it needs more frequent cleaning to maintain adequate flow rate — the filtration-quality gain comes with a real maintenance-frequency cost, not just a purchase-price one.
What sand actually misses
Sand's coarser 20–40 micron range lets some very fine particles pass through that a cartridge or DE filter would catch — in practice this rarely causes a visible clarity problem for most pools, but it's the honest reason sand is sometimes described as giving "good" rather than "excellent" water clarity.
When the difference actually shows
The gap between filter types is most noticeable in pools with heavy bather load, nearby dust or pollen, or an ongoing algae or clarity issue — a lightly used, well-maintained pool often looks equally clear regardless of which filter type is behind it.

Filter media technology worth knowing about

A few developments in filter media are worth understanding before buying, since they change the maintenance and performance profile of an otherwise standard filter type.

Glass media as a sand alternative

Some sand filter systems now use crushed recycled glass instead of traditional sand — it filters slightly finer, needs backwashing less often, and lasts longer before replacement. Costs more upfront than standard sand but can work out cheaper over several years given the reduced replacement frequency.

Reusable DE alternative cartridges

Some cartridge products are designed to approximate DE-level filtration without needing loose DE powder at all, aiming to combine cartridge convenience with finer filtration. Performance varies by brand — worth reading recent, specific reviews for a given product rather than assuming all "DE-alternative" cartridges perform identically.

Antimicrobial-treated cartridge material

Some cartridge filters now use fabric treated to resist mold and bacterial growth within the cartridge itself, extending the interval between deep cleanings. A genuine incremental improvement rather than a reason to skip regular rinsing altogether.

Signs your current filter needs attention

Filter pressure gauge reading high

Pressure rising 8–10 psi above your clean baseline reading means the filter media has caught enough debris to restrict flow — time to backwash (sand/DE) or clean/replace the cartridge. This is expected, normal filter behavior, not a fault. If the gauge isn't reading at all or never seems to change, see our pressure gauge troubleshooting guide.

Cloudy water despite balanced chemistry

If chemistry tests fine but the pool still looks dull or hazy, the filter itself is often the actual bottleneck — either overdue for cleaning, undersized for the pool, or (for cartridges) worn out and no longer trapping fine particles effectively. See our cloudy water guide for the full diagnostic order.

Sand or DE media past its working life

Sand filter media compacts and loses effectiveness over years of use even with regular backwashing — if pressure keeps climbing fast after cleaning despite everything else checking out, aging media is a likely cause. Sand typically needs replacing every 5–7 years, DE grids somewhat less often with proper care.

How backwashing actually works

Backwashing (used for sand and DE filters, not cartridge) reverses normal water flow through the filter to flush trapped debris out through a waste line, rather than back into the pool. Getting the sequence right matters — doing it out of order can push debris back into the pool instead of out the waste line.

  1. Turn off the pump first

    Never move a multiport or push-pull valve while the pump is running — this can damage the valve internals, which is one of the more expensive filter repairs to fix.

  2. Set the valve to backwash

    This reverses flow direction through the filter and routes it out to the designated waste line instead of back to the pool.

  3. Run until the sight glass runs clear

    Most systems have a clear sight glass in the waste line — run backwash mode until water passing through it looks clear rather than cloudy, typically 2–3 minutes.

  4. Rinse for 30-60 seconds

    A separate "rinse" valve setting resettles the filter media properly before returning to normal filtration — skipping this step can send a cloud of fine debris back into the pool the moment normal filtering resumes.

  5. Return to filter mode

    For a DE filter specifically, this is also the point to recharge with fresh DE powder through the skimmer, per your filter's rated DE capacity.

Cartridge filters skip this whole process — instead, the cartridge is removed, hosed down (or soaked in a cartridge cleaner solution for a deeper periodic clean), and reinstalled. This is the main hands-on maintenance trade-off between the filter types discussed above.

Mistakes that shorten a filter's working life

Backwashing on a schedule instead of by pressure

Backwashing too often wastes water and can actually reduce filtration quality — sand and DE media both filter better once they've built up a light layer of trapped debris. Go by the 8–10 psi pressure rise rule rather than a fixed calendar schedule.

Letting a cartridge go too long between cleanings

A heavily clogged cartridge restricts flow, forcing the pump to work harder and reducing turnover — by the time it's visibly gray or won't rinse clean, it's often overdue by weeks, not days.

Ignoring persistent high pressure after cleaning

If pressure stays high immediately after backwashing or cleaning, the media itself may be worn out (compacted sand, degraded cartridge pleats) rather than simply dirty — see our pump and filter troubleshooting guide for the full diagnostic path.

Mismatching filter size to pump output

A filter rated below your pump's actual flow rate gets pushed harder than intended, shortening media life and reducing filtration quality — always check both figures together when replacing either the pump or the filter, not just the one you're buying.

Environmental and water-use considerations

Filter choice has a real environmental footprint beyond just the filter itself, worth weighing alongside performance and cost — particularly for owners in drought-prone or water-restricted regions.

Backwash water use
Sand and DE filters both discharge a meaningful volume of water to waste with every backwash — depending on backwash frequency and local water cost, this adds up over a season and is a genuine, often-overlooked factor in some regions' water restrictions specifically targeting pool backwashing during drought periods.
DE powder disposal
Spent DE powder from backwashing needs to be disposed of according to local regulations rather than simply washed to a storm drain, since it can affect local waterways — check municipal guidance, as rules vary meaningfully by location and some areas require it be swept up and bagged rather than backwashed to waste at all.
Cartridge waste
A used cartridge is a solid waste item replaced every 1–2 years rather than an ongoing water-use concern — some manufacturers and specialty retailers offer recycling programs for old cartridges, worth checking before simply discarding one.
Recapture systems
Some owners in water-restricted areas install a backwash recapture system that settles out debris and allows water to be reused rather than sent entirely to waste — a bigger upfront investment, but worth investigating for a sand or DE filter in a region where water cost or restriction is a real constraint.

New pool vs replacing an existing filter

The buying decision looks a little different depending on which situation you're in, and it's worth being clear about which one applies before choosing a product.

New pool, no existing equipment

You have a genuinely open choice between all three filter types — this is the point to weigh maintenance effort, running cost, and filtration quality against each other without the constraint of matching existing plumbing or a pump that's already sized for a specific filter type.

Replacing a failed or worn-out filter, same type

The simplest replacement path — matching your existing filter type and flow rating avoids re-plumbing and keeps the swap straightforward, usually a same-day job for anyone reasonably comfortable with basic pool equipment work.

Switching filter types on an existing pool

Possible, but budget for re-plumbing work around the filter tank connections, and double check your existing pump's flow rate suits the new filter type's requirements — a pump sized well for a sand filter isn't automatically well-matched to a DE filter's different flow characteristics.

Common questions

Which filter type is actually best?

DE filters catch the finest particles and give the clearest water, cartridge filters offer the best balance of filtration quality and easy maintenance, and sand filters are the lowest-effort and cheapest to run. There's no single best filter — it's a genuine trade-off between filtration fineness, maintenance effort, and running cost.

How often should I clean or replace my filter?

Cartridge filters need rinsing every 2–4 weeks and full replacement every 1–2 years. Sand filters get backwashed when pressure rises 8–10 psi above clean baseline, roughly every 4–6 weeks, with sand media replaced every 5–7 years. DE filters get backwashed similarly to sand, with fresh DE powder recharged after every backwash.

Can I switch from sand to cartridge, or between filter types?

Generally yes if the new filter is correctly sized for your pump's flow rate, though it usually means replacing the whole filter tank rather than converting an existing one — the internal design differs enough between types that conversion kits are rare and situational.

Why is my filter pressure gauge reading high?

Rising pressure above your clean baseline means the filter media is catching debris and needs cleaning or backwashing — this is normal filter behavior, not a fault. Our pump and filter troubleshooting guide covers what to do if pressure stays high even right after cleaning.
