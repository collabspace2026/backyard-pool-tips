Best Pool Pump for Inground Pools

Single-speed vs variable-speed, and when the energy savings genuinely stack up.

The payback math, worked through properly

This is where variable-speed pumps genuinely earn their higher price tag — unlike smaller above-ground pools (see our above-ground pump guide), inground systems typically run longer daily cycles on bigger pumps, which is exactly the situation where variable speed pays for itself fastest.

A single-speed inground pump commonly draws around 1.5 kWh running flat-out, with no way to slow down. Running 8 hours a day at a typical residential rate of $0.16/kWh, that's about $1.92 a day — roughly $700 a year in electricity for the pump alone.

A variable-speed pump doing the same job at a lower, filtration-appropriate speed can draw as little as 0.3–0.5 kWh average across that same runtime — around $0.38–0.64 a day, or roughly $140–230 a year. That's a saving of $470–560 a year. Against a variable-speed pump that typically costs $400–700 more upfront than a comparable single-speed unit, the payback period lands around 1–1.5 years. Every year after that is straightforward savings.

Sizing for your plumbing

Inground pools are plumbed with wider, longer pipe runs than above-ground setups — commonly 1.5" or 2" pipe. Pipe diameter caps how much flow you can actually push through without excessive friction loss, noise, and wasted energy, regardless of how powerful the pump is. As a rough guide:

1.5" plumbing
Most single-speed and variable-speed pumps in the 1–1.5 HP range are a reasonable match.
2" plumbing
Can support higher-flow pumps, useful for larger pools or ones with water features and spa jets that need extra flow on demand.

Buying a pump rated well beyond what your existing pipe can handle doesn't get you more performance — it just adds noise and pushes water through undersized pipe less efficiently. If you're unsure what you're plumbed with, a pool professional can check in a few minutes.

Rebates and regulations are worth checking

Several US states and utilities now require or heavily incentivize variable-speed pumps for new pool construction and pump replacements, and many electricity providers offer rebates that can meaningfully offset the upfront price difference. Check your local utility's website before buying — a rebate can shorten an already-short payback period even further, and in some areas a single-speed replacement pump may not be compliant with current rules at all.

What else is worth checking beyond speed

Plumbing unions
Confirm the new pump matches your current pipe size and connection type before ordering.
Automation & salt compatibility
Most modern variable-speed pumps play well with common automation controllers and salt chlorinators, but it's worth confirming for anything more than a few years old.
Warranty length
Variable-speed pumps commonly carry longer warranties (2–3 years) than budget single-speed units, reflecting the more complex electronics inside.
Noise at low speed
A genuinely variable-speed pump is noticeably quieter running its long daily filtration cycle at low RPM than a single-speed pump running flat-out for the same job.

How long should an inground pump last?

A well-maintained inground pump — single-speed or variable-speed — commonly runs 8–12 years before the motor needs replacing, longer than typical above-ground pumps thanks to heavier-duty construction and the equipment usually sitting in a proper enclosure out of direct weather. Variable-speed pumps also tend to run cooler at their lower everyday speeds, which can extend motor life further. If a pump older than that starts showing the symptoms covered in our pump and filter troubleshooting guide, it's often more cost-effective to replace it — parts for a pump nearing end of life can add up fast, and a new variable-speed unit will usually pay part of its own cost back in electricity savings regardless.

Dual-speed vs true variable-speed — what's the real difference?

A dual-speed pump (like the AQUASTRONG above) gives you two fixed speeds to choose from — typically a high setting for cleaning or spa jets, and a low setting for everyday filtration. A true variable-speed pump gives you a full range, letting you dial in the exact speed your pool actually needs rather than picking the closer of two presets. That fuller range is where most of the extra efficiency comes from: running at, say, 40% of a pump's flow uses roughly 6–8% of the power a single fixed high speed would, since pump power draw drops with roughly the cube of speed. A dual-speed pump's fixed "low" setting is rarely tuned quite that precisely to your specific pool, so it captures some of the saving but not all of it — which is exactly why the payback math earlier in this guide assumes true variable-speed, not dual-speed.

Common questions

Is a variable-speed pump really worth the extra cost for an inground pool?

In most cases, yes — typical payback is 1–2 years from electricity savings alone, and the pump keeps saving money for years after that on a system that usually runs 8+ hours a day.

Do I need an electrician to install a variable-speed pump?

Many variable-speed pumps use standard 115/230V wiring compatible with an existing single-speed connection, but requirements vary by model — check the specific pump's electrical spec before assuming a straight swap.

Will a variable-speed pump work with my existing automation or salt system?

Most modern variable-speed pumps are compatible with common pool automation and salt chlorination systems, but older automation controllers can be an exception — check compatibility before buying.

How low can I run the speed and still keep the pool clean?

Lower than most people expect. Filtration needs enough time, not high flow, so many owners run an extended low-speed cycle (10+ hours) at a fraction of the flow rate a single-speed pump would use, and the water stays just as clean.
