Why Does My Pool Smell Like Chlorine?

The common belief has it backwards — a strong chemical smell means too little effective chlorine, not too much.

The myth we hear constantly at the shop counter

"The pool smells really strongly of chlorine, so I've been adding less" is one of the most common — and most backwards — things we hear. The strong, sharp, eye-stinging chemical smell people associate with "too much chlorine" is almost never caused by too much chlorine at all. It's caused by too little of the right kind, and cutting back on dosing usually makes the actual problem worse, not better.

What's really happening: chloramines

Chlorine in pool water exists in two forms that matter here: free chlorine, which is the active sanitising chlorine actually available to do its job, and combined chlorine (chloramines), which is chlorine that's already reacted with ammonia and nitrogen compounds — from sweat, urine, sunscreen, body oils, and other organic contaminants — and is now essentially used up. Chloramines are what produce that harsh, sharp smell and the eye and skin irritation people blame on "too much chlorine." Properly balanced water with adequate free chlorine and low combined chlorine has comparatively little smell at all — a truly well-sanitised pool doesn't smell strongly of chemicals, despite what most people assume.

Total chlorine is simply free chlorine plus combined chlorine added together. Testing kits that only report total chlorine can mask the real problem, since a pool could show an adequate total chlorine number while most of it is actually combined chloramines rather than the active free chlorine doing any sanitising work.

Why it happens

Chloramines form whenever there isn't enough free chlorine to fully oxidize the organic contaminants entering the water. A few common contributors:

  • Heavy bather load — more people means more sweat, sunscreen, and body oils introduced per hour than routine chlorine dosing accounts for.
  • Infrequent shocking — routine daily dosing maintains a baseline but doesn't clear an accumulating chloramine load the way a proper shock does. See our how to shock a pool guide for the difference.
  • Poor circulation or filtration — contaminants that aren't being effectively filtered and treated stick around longer, giving chloramines more opportunity to build up.
  • Not showering before swimming — a significant, often-overlooked source. Sweat, lotion, sunscreen, and cosmetic residue rinsed off before entering the pool is contaminant load that never has the chance to react with chlorine in the first place.
  • Limited ventilation (indoor pools especially) — outdoor pools benefit from open air and UV exposure that help break down chloramine gas at the surface. Indoor pools trap it, which is exactly why indoor pool facilities often have that distinctive, much stronger "pool smell" that outdoor pools rarely match.

How to actually test for it

A basic test strip that only reports total chlorine won't tell you whether you have a chloramine problem — you need free chlorine and total chlorine tested separately, then calculate combined chlorine as the difference between them (total minus free). A combined chlorine reading above roughly 0.2–0.5 ppm is generally considered a sign of a chloramine buildup worth addressing, even if total chlorine looks technically adequate on a basic strip. A proper liquid test kit, or a test from a pool shop, typically gives you free and combined chlorine as separate readings rather than requiring the subtraction yourself.

The fix: breakpoint shock

Clearing an existing chloramine problem needs a shock dose calculated to reach breakpoint chlorination — high enough to fully destroy the chloramines already in the water, not just add more routine chlorine on top of them. As a rough rule of thumb, that's often around 10 times your combined chlorine reading in added free chlorine — a pool reading 0.5 ppm combined chlorine might need a dose calculated to add roughly 5 ppm of free chlorine to genuinely break through, well beyond a routine maintenance dose. Our how to shock a pool guide covers the full process, dosing guidance, and the mistakes that cause staining or wasted product.

Simply adding a bit more chlorine each day without reaching that breakpoint threshold tends to keep the problem circling rather than actually resolving it — this is exactly the "adding more chlorine but the smell won't go away" experience a lot of pool owners describe, and it's a dosing problem rather than a sign that chlorine itself isn't working.

Why you can still smell "chlorine" on your skin after a well-balanced pool

This is a genuinely common and slightly confusing follow-up question: even after fixing a chloramine problem, some people still notice a chlorine-like smell on their skin or hair after swimming, even in water that tests fine. Part of this is simply residual chlorine itself, which is a normal and expected result of swimming in properly sanitised water — it's not the same sharp, irritating chloramine smell, just a milder chemical note that a quick shower after swimming clears easily. The other part is that skin oils and residue react with even well-balanced chlorine levels on contact, producing a faint version of the same chemical compounds in smaller quantities. Neither is a sign of a problem with the water — it's simply a normal side effect of chlorine doing its job, and rinsing off after swimming (not just before) deals with it completely.

A worked example of the breakpoint calculation

To make the "10 times combined chlorine" rule of thumb concrete: say a pool tests at 2.0 ppm total chlorine and 1.5 ppm free chlorine. Combined chlorine is the difference — 0.5 ppm. Using the rough 10x guideline, that pool needs a shock dose calculated to add roughly 5 ppm of free chlorine to reach breakpoint, not just a routine top-up dose. For context, a typical routine maintenance shock (see our how to shock a pool guide for full dosing detail) is often calculated to add somewhere around 5–10 ppm depending on product concentration and pool volume — so a combined chlorine reading in that 0.5 ppm range is right at the edge of what a normal shock dose can handle, while a higher combined chlorine reading needs a proportionally bigger dose to actually clear it. This is exactly why testing combined chlorine specifically, not just total chlorine, matters before deciding how much shock to use.

Public pools vs backyard pools

If you've ever noticed that a commercial or public pool sometimes has a much stronger smell than a well-run backyard pool, that's not your imagination — public pools deal with dramatically higher bather loads relative to their water volume, meaning far more sweat, sunscreen, and body-care residue entering the water per hour than almost any residential pool sees. Commercial facilities manage this with more frequent, higher-volume chlorination and dedicated ventilation systems for indoor venues specifically because chloramine buildup is a constant, ongoing challenge at that scale. A backyard pool with normal bather load and a reasonable shocking schedule genuinely shouldn't smell anything like a busy public pool — if it does, that's a stronger signal something in the routine (shocking frequency, bather load, circulation) needs attention.

Preventing it from coming back

Shower before swimming
The single most underrated prevention step. A quick rinse removes a meaningful share of the sweat, sunscreen, and body oils that would otherwise become chloramine-forming contaminant load.
Shock on a regular schedule
Weekly to every other week for a regularly used pool clears chloramines before they build up to a noticeable level, rather than letting them accumulate until the smell becomes obvious.
Maintain adequate circulation
Enough daily filter run time and good circulation keeps contaminants moving through the filtration system rather than lingering in the water.
Ventilate indoor pools properly
Good air exchange above an indoor pool surface reduces how much chloramine gas accumulates in the air, which is a large part of what makes indoor pool smell so much more noticeable than outdoor.

When it's genuinely something else

Occasionally a chemical smell isn't chloramines at all — a musty or earthy smell can point to algae even before it's visibly green (see our green pool guide), and a sharp, unusual chemical odor that doesn't match typical chlorine smell could indicate a genuine water chemistry imbalance or a contamination source unrelated to bather load. If a proper breakpoint shock and a few days of normal maintenance don't resolve the smell, that's worth a professional water test rather than continuing to guess — home test kits are good for routine monitoring but can drift out of calibration or miss less common issues a shop-grade test would catch.

Common questions

Does a strong chlorine smell mean the pool is over-chlorinated?

No — it almost always means the opposite. A strong smell comes from chloramines, which form when there isn't enough free chlorine to fully oxidize contaminants. Properly chlorinated water at correct levels has very little smell at all.

Why do indoor pools smell stronger than outdoor pools?

Indoor pools trap chloramine gas that escapes from the water surface, since there's no open air and UV exposure to help break it down the way outdoor pools naturally get. Ventilation matters much more for indoor pools as a result.

Will showering before swimming really make a difference?

Yes, meaningfully. Sweat, sunscreen, lotion, and body oils are major sources of the ammonia and nitrogen compounds that react with chlorine to form chloramines. A quick rinse before swimming measurably reduces how much of that load enters the water in the first place.

How long does it take for the smell to go away after fixing it?

Often within a few hours of a proper breakpoint shock and continued filtration, though a severe or long-standing chloramine problem can take a day or two to fully clear as the filter removes the byproducts.
